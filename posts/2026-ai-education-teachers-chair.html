AI in education, from the teacher's chair

What I'm actually trying to teach, and what the tools threaten

June 2026

Most conversations about AI in education start with cheating. Can students get a model to write the essay, solve the problem set, generate the analysis, and how do we catch them. I teach, and I mentor students through research projects, and I'll admit that's not the part that keeps me up. Plagiarism is an old problem with a new engine. The thing I find genuinely worth thinking carefully about is quieter, and it has to do with what teaching is for in the first place.

When I teach a field course, or walk a student through their first real research project, the deliverable: the lab report, the analysis, the paper, is not the point. It's a byproduct. What I'm actually trying to build is judgment: the ability to look at a messy result and tell whether it's real, to generate a hypothesis and then genuinely try to kill it, to sit inside a hard problem long enough to understand it rather than just resolve it. That capacity doesn't transfer through explanation. It's built by doing the work, including — especially — the parts that are frustrating and slow.

Learning science has a name for this that I keep coming back to: desirable difficulties. The friction in learning is often not an obstacle to it; it frequently is it. The struggle to structure a messy dataset, the dead ends in a literature search, the discomfort of not knowing whether your interpretation holds: those aren't inefficiencies to be optimized away. They're the reps that build the muscle. Which is exactly why a tool that removes the friction so smoothly is worth taking seriously, and not mainly as a cheating risk.

An LLM is extraordinarily good at producing the artifacts of intellectual work while letting the student skip the work itself. It will hand back a clean analysis, a fluent interpretation, a plausible discussion section, the visible outputs of someone who has wrestled with a problem, without the student having done any wrestling. And because the output looks like the genuine article, it's easy for everyone, the student included, to mistake having the artifact for having the understanding. The most worrying thing a student can hand me isn't sloppy work. It's work that's too clean, an analysis with none of the fingerprints of having actually grappled with the data, a confident conclusion the student can't defend when I ask the second question.

I want to be careful not to slide into doom here, because I don't believe in it. Banning the tools is neither possible nor wise, and pretending researchers don't use them is fantasy. I use them. A student who graduates unable to work alongside these systems is not better prepared; they're worse prepared. The failure mode isn't using the tool. It's using it as an oracle: accepting confident, fluent output without the judgment to know when it's wrong. And that judgment is precisely what the unsupervised use of the tool prevents them from developing. It's a loop, and the teacher's job is to break it.

So in practice the job shifts, and I think it shifts in a direction that's actually good for teaching. The first thing I try to teach now is skepticism aimed at the tool itself; the same not-fooling-yourself discipline I'd want them to apply to their own results, turned on the confident assistant. A model's output is a draft to be verified, not an answer to be trusted; learning to check it, to find where it's subtly wrong, is itself a rigorous and teachable skill, and a more transferable one than the task it short-circuits. The second thing is to move the weight of assessment onto the parts that can't be outsourced: the reasoning, defended out loud; the choices behind an analysis, explained; the "show me why you believe this," asked in person. When the deliverable is the thinking rather than the document, the document a model can produce stops being the prize.

And used well, this is the part I'm genuinely optimistic about, the same tool can serve the difficulty instead of removing it. A model makes a tireless sparring partner for testing whether you actually understand something: explain your reasoning and have it probe the weak points, ask it to argue the opposing case, use it to generate the hard follow-up questions a good mentor would ask when a mentor isn't in the room. Pointed at the student's understanding rather than at the student's assignment, it can create productive difficulty rather than dissolve it. The difference is entirely in what you aim it at, and teaching that aim is, increasingly, the work.

What I want a student to leave with hasn't really changed. I want them to be able to look at a confident, fluent, authoritative-sounding result; whether it came from a model, from a paper, or from their own first instinct — and have the judgment to sense when it's wrong and the discipline to check. That capacity is more valuable now than it has ever been, not less, precisely because fluent and authoritative is so cheap to manufacture. The tools haven't made that kind of teaching obsolete. If anything they've made it the whole game.

— David Henderson
