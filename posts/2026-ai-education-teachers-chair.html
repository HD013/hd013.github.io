AI in education, from the teacher's chair

What I'm trying to teach, and what the tools threaten

July 21, 2026

Most conversations about AI in education start with cheating. Can students get a model to write the essay, solve the problem set or generate the analysis, and how do we catch them? I teach, and I mentor students through research projects, and I'll admit that's not the part that keeps me up. Plagiarism is an old problem with a new engine. The question I find more important is subtler, and it has to do with what teaching is for in the first place.

When I teach a field course, or walk a student through their first research project, the deliverable (the lab report, the analysis, the paper) is a byproduct, not the point. What I'm trying to build is judgment. That means the ability to look at a messy result and tell whether it's real, to generate a hypothesis and then make a serious attempt to kill it, and to stay inside a hard problem long enough to understand it rather than just resolve it. That capacity doesn't transfer through explanation. It's built by doing the work, especially the parts that are frustrating and slow.

Learning science has a name for this that I keep coming back to: desirable difficulties. The friction in learning is often not an obstacle to it, and frequently it is the learning. Structuring a messy dataset, hitting dead ends in a literature search, sitting with the discomfort of not knowing whether your interpretation holds. Those aren't inefficiencies to be optimized away. They're the reps that build the muscle. That is why a tool that removes the friction so smoothly deserves serious attention, and not mainly as a cheating risk.

An LLM is extraordinarily good at producing the artifacts of intellectual work while letting the student skip the work itself. It will hand back a clean analysis, a fluent interpretation, a plausible discussion section. These are the visible outputs of someone who has wrestled with a problem, produced without the student doing any wrestling. And because the output looks authentic, it's easy for everyone, the student included, to mistake having the artifact for having the understanding. The most worrying work a student can hand me isn't sloppy. It's too clean: an analysis with none of the fingerprints of having grappled with the data, or a confident conclusion the student can't defend when I ask the second question.

I want to be careful not to slide into doom here, because I don't believe in it. Banning the tools is neither possible nor wise, and pretending researchers don't use them is fantasy. I use them. A student who graduates unable to work alongside these systems isn't better prepared. They're worse prepared. The failure mode is using it as an oracle, accepting confident, fluent output without the judgment to know when it's wrong. And that judgment is what unsupervised use of the tool prevents them from developing. It's a loop, and the teacher's job is to break it.

So in practice the job shifts, and I think it shifts in a direction that's good for teaching. The first change is that I now teach skepticism aimed at the tool itself. It's the same discipline of not fooling yourself that I'd want students to apply to their own results, turned on a confident assistant. A model's output is a draft to be verified, not an answer to be trusted. Learning to check it, and to find where it's subtly wrong, is a rigorous and teachable skill in its own right, and a more transferable one than the task it short-circuits. The second change is moving the weight of assessment onto the parts that can't be outsourced. Students defend their reasoning out loud, explain the choices behind an analysis, and answer "show me why you believe this" in person. When the deliverable is the thinking rather than the document, the document a model can produce stops being the prize.

Used well, the same tool can serve the difficulty instead of removing it, and this is the part I'm optimistic about. A model makes a tireless sparring partner for testing whether you understand something. Explain your reasoning and have it probe the weak points. Ask it to argue the opposing case. Use it to generate the hard follow-up questions a good mentor would ask when no mentor is in the room. Pointed at the student's understanding rather than the student's assignment, it can create productive difficulty instead of dissolving it. The difference lies entirely in what you aim it at, and teaching that aim is increasingly the work.

What I want a student to leave with hasn't changed. I want them to be able to look at a confident, fluent, authoritative-sounding result, whether it came from a model, a paper or their own first instinct, and have the judgment to sense when it's wrong and the discipline to check. That capacity matters more now than it ever has, because fluent and authoritative is so cheap to manufacture. The tools haven't made that kind of teaching obsolete. If anything, they've made it the whole game.

David Henderson, Ph.D.
